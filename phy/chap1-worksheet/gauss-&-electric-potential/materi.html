Gauss's Law & Electric Potential

Two ideas, one story: counting invisible field lines through a surface, and mapping the electric "height" that makes charges want to move. No heavy math — just patterns, pictures, and small steps.

Field lines crossing the dashed surface: 8 out — all counted, no matter its exact shape.
1
Warm-up recap

What is an Electric Field?

Before flux and Gauss's Law can make sense, we need one picture firmly in mind: the electric field as a set of arrows filling space around a charge.

An electric field is a way of describing how a charge affects the space around it, even before anything else is there. Instead of drawing forces on every possible test charge, we simplify life by drawing field lines — arrows that show the direction a small positive charge would be pushed at that point.

Three rules for field lines 1. Lines start on positive charges and end on negative charges (or head off to infinity).
2. Lines never cross each other.
3. Where lines are packed close together, the field is stronger. Where they spread out, the field is weaker.
Check yourself

Two diagrams show field lines around a charge. In diagram A, the lines are close together. In diagram B, the same number of lines are spread far apart. Which region has the stronger field?

Diagram A. Line density (how packed they are) tells you field strength — not how many lines you drew in total.
2
Building the tool

Electric Flux — Counting Lines Through a Window

Flux just means: "how much field passes through a surface." Nothing more mysterious than that.

Analogy Imagine standing at an open window on a windy day. If the window faces straight into the wind, a lot of air rushes through. If you turn the window sideways to the wind, almost no air passes through — even though the wind hasn't changed at all. Electric flux works the same way, except it's field lines passing through a surface instead of wind through a window.
General formula
Φ = E · A · cos θ
Φ (phi) is flux, E is field strength, A is the surface area, and θ is the angle between the field and the surface's perpendicular ("normal") direction. When the field hits the surface straight-on, θ = 0° and cos θ = 1, so Φ = E·A — the simplest case.
Worked example
  1. A uniform field of E = 500 N/C passes straight through a flat surface of area A = 2 m², perpendicular to it (θ = 0°).
  2. Since θ = 0°, cos θ = 1, so we can use Φ = E × A.
  3. Φ = 500 × 2 = 1000 N·m²/C.
Common mistake Forgetting the cos θ term when the surface is tilted. If a problem doesn't say "perpendicular" or "face-on," check the angle before plugging numbers in.
Check yourself

A flat surface is turned so it's exactly edge-on to a field (θ = 90°). What is the flux through it?

Zero. Since cos 90° = 0, no field lines actually pass through the surface — they slide along it instead.
3
The big idea

Gauss's Law

Gauss's Law is one simple sentence: the total flux out of any closed surface only depends on the charge trapped inside it.

Analogy Picture a closed fishing net wrapped all the way around part of a lake. It doesn't matter if the net is shaped like a ball, a box, or a lumpy potato — only the fish actually inside the net get caught. Charge outside the surface contributes nothing to the total flux, no matter how close it is.
Gauss's Law
Φtotal = Qenc / ε₀
Φtotal is the total flux through a closed surface, Qenc is the total charge enclosed inside that surface, and ε₀ (epsilon-naught) is a fixed constant, ε₀ ≈ 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²).
Key idea The shape of the closed surface (called a "Gaussian surface") never matters. We are always free to imagine any convenient closed shape — usually a sphere, cylinder, or box — because Gauss's Law only cares about the charge trapped inside.
Check yourself

If you double the amount of charge enclosed inside a Gaussian surface, what happens to the total flux through that surface?

It also doubles. Φtotal = Qenc/ε₀ is a direct (linear) relationship — flux scales exactly with enclosed charge.
4
Putting it to work

Using Gauss's Law: Field of a Point Charge

Gauss's Law becomes genuinely useful when we pick a Gaussian surface with a lot of symmetry. For a single point charge, that's a sphere.

Step-by-step reasoning
  1. Draw an imaginary sphere of radius r centered on the point charge Q.
  2. By symmetry, the field E has the same strength everywhere on this sphere, and always points straight outward — perpendicular to the surface (θ = 0°, so cos θ = 1).
  3. The surface area of a sphere is A = 4πr².
  4. So the total flux is simply Φ = E × 4πr².
  5. Gauss's Law says this must equal Q/ε₀. Setting them equal: E × 4πr² = Q/ε₀.
  6. Solving for E: E = Q / (4πε₀r²).
Field of a point charge
E = kQ / r²   where   k = 1/(4πε₀) ≈ 9 × 10⁹ N·m²/C²
This is the same formula you may already know from Coulomb's Law — Gauss's Law simply gives us another route to the same answer, and a much faster one for symmetric shapes.
Worked example
  1. Find the field at r = 0.3 m from a point charge Q = 2 × 10⁻⁶ C.
  2. E = kQ/r² = (9×10⁹ × 2×10⁻⁶) / (0.3)²
  3. Numerator: 9×10⁹ × 2×10⁻⁶ = 1.8 × 10⁴
  4. Denominator: 0.3² = 0.09
  5. E = 1.8×10⁴ / 0.09 = 2 × 10⁵ N/C
Check yourself

If you move to a point twice as far away from the same charge (r doubles), what happens to E?

E becomes 4 times smaller. Because E ∝ 1/r², doubling r divides E by 2² = 4.
5
Changing viewpoint

From Field to Potential: Electric "Height"

Fields tell us about force. Potential tells us about energy — and energy is often easier to work with because it has no direction to track.

Analogy Think about a ball sitting on a hill. The higher up the hill, the more gravitational potential energy it has. Roll it, and that stored energy turns into motion. Electric charges behave the same way inside a field: a charge at a location with more electric potential energy will "roll" toward lower potential energy if it's free to move.

Just like we divide gravitational PE by mass to get a property of the location alone (height), we divide electric potential energy by charge to get electric potential — a property of the location in the field, independent of whatever charge happens to be sitting there.

Definition of electric potential
V = PE / q
V is measured in volts (V), where 1 volt = 1 joule per coulomb. Potential belongs to a point in space, the same way elevation belongs to a spot on a map — not to whichever hiker happens to be standing there.
6
A useful formula

Potential of a Point Charge

Just like the field, the potential around a point charge follows a clean formula — but this one is simpler, because potential has no direction.

Potential of a point charge
V = kQ / r
Notice E ∝ 1/r² but V ∝ 1/r — potential falls off more gently with distance. And because V is a scalar (just a number, no direction), adding up the potential from several charges is simple addition — no angles required.
Worked example
  1. Find V at r = 0.5 m from a charge Q = 3 × 10⁻⁶ C.
  2. V = kQ/r = (9×10⁹ × 3×10⁻⁶) / 0.5
  3. Numerator: 9×10⁹ × 3×10⁻⁶ = 2.7 × 10⁴
  4. V = 2.7×10⁴ / 0.5 = 5.4 × 10⁴ V
Check yourself

Two point charges of equal size sit at different distances from you. Which one contributes more to the potential you experience — the near one or the far one?

The near one. Since V ∝ 1/r, a smaller r gives a larger contribution to V.
7
Seeing the whole picture

Equipotential Surfaces — Contour Maps of Electric Height

If potential is like elevation, then equipotential surfaces are exactly like the contour lines on a hiking map.

Analogy On a hiking map, a contour line connects every point at the same elevation. Walking along a contour line means staying at the same height — no climbing, no descending, no work done against gravity. An equipotential surface connects every point in space that has the same electric potential. Moving a charge along it takes zero work.
Key idea Field lines always cross equipotential surfaces at a perfect right angle (90°). This makes sense: the field points in the direction of steepest change in potential — exactly like water always flows straight downhill, perpendicular to the contour lines, never along them.
